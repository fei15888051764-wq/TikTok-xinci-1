Blud is just bro with a passport. It comes from British street slang, specifically Caribbean-influenced English in London, and it means friend, brother, or guy. On TikTok it has become a universal term of address. You will see Americans who have never been east of Ohio calling each other blud in comments. The internet does that. It takes a regional word, strips the context, and hands it to everyone with a WiFi connection.

It is not offensive. It is just a little goofy when used wrong. I first heard "blud" in UK grime TikToks around 2022. British creators used it naturally as a term of address, and American viewers immediately started copying it in comments. The crossover moment was when non-British creators started using it in their own skits, but with the wrong intonation. You could tell who was faking.

## Where did blud come from?

The word has roots in Jamaican patois, where "blood" is used to refer to family or close friends, people who might as well be blood relatives, and it migrated to UK urban slang in the 2000s, particularly in grime and drill music, where you would hear it in tracks from South London artists who used it as naturally as breathing. You would hear "what is going on, blud" in tracks from South London artists. It was specific. It had geography.

Then TikTok found it. Around 2022, British creators started using "blud" in skits and reaction videos. American audiences picked it up because it sounded cooler than "bro" and slightly more aggressive than "dude." By 2024 it was standard internet vocabulary. The original accent did not make the trip. Now it is just "blud," flat and Americanized.

## The problem with blud

Here is where it gets messy. If you are not Black, not British, and not from the communities that birthed this word, your usage is basically costume. It is not a slur. Nobody is going to cancel you for saying "blud" in a TikTok comment. But the word carries a specific cultural weight that does not survive the Atlantic crossing intact. Using it to sound edgy is cringe. Using it because you heard it in a KSI video and thought it sounded cool is even more cringe.

> "Blud really said he is 'locked in' at the gym and then ate a whole pizza. Respect."

> @username: blud thinks he can cook 💀
> @reply: he burned water

The best usage is observational. You are not addressing someone as blud. You are talking about them. "Blud over here thinks he is the main character." It creates distance and comedy. Direct address is where it usually falls apart. If you are British, ignore everything I just said. You earned it.
