"Chronically online" is not a medical diagnosis. It is a roast. The phrase describes someone who has been melted by the algorithm to the point where they reference memes in casual conversation, start arguments about fictional characters, or genuinely believe Twitter is representative of real life. You know the type. They say "unalived" instead of died, call a sandwich a "sammie," and have strong feelings about emoji usage. Being online a lot is not the problem. It is when your brain gets rewired by the feed to the point where you cannot tell a trending hashtag from an actual human emotion. That is chronically online.

## Where did this even come from?

The term started on Twitter around 2019, picked up steam on TikTok in 2021, and by 2023 it was everywhere. I first saw it in a reply to someone who'd written a 12-tweet thread analyzing the psychology of a TikTok housewife who folds towels. The reply was simply: "chronically online behavior." Nothing else. No follow-up. Just that. And it stung because it was true. The phrase spread because it named something everyone recognized but did not have language for. Before this, we just called people "terminally online," but that sounded too clinical, whereas "chronically online" lands with the precision of a diagnosis you did not ask for but absolutely deserved.

## The chronically online starter pack

You will see it in the comments of any video where someone takes a meme too seriously. "This is literally me fr fr." Bro, it is a video of a cat. Calm down. The chronically online person treats every minor inconvenience like a systemic failure and then writes three paragraphs about it in a group chat that was supposed to be about brunch plans. They have strong feelings about emoji usage. They think the thumbs-up emoji is passive-aggressive. They think punctuation in texts is hostile. They have opinions about font choices in Instagram stories.

I saw a TikTok last month where a girl explained that using the thumbs-up emoji is passive-aggressive. The video went viral. The comments were a war zone. That is chronically online. It is not about screen time. It is about losing the ability to gauge what matters offline. It is about treating internet etiquette like it is real etiquette. It is about forgetting that most people do not care about any of this.

> @username: this is literally me fr fr

A girl posts a video explaining that the thumbs-up emoji is passive-aggressive. Top comment: "chronically online take." That is how the word gets used. It is a diagnosis and a dismissal in one.

## Not everyone's a fan

Some people push back on the term because it gets used as a blanket dismissal. You critique a movie? Chronically online. You know too much about a niche topic? Chronically online. The phrase can become a way to mock anyone who cares about anything. Which is annoying. There is a difference between being deeply online and simply being informed. The problem is that line keeps moving, and what counted as deeply online behavior in 2024 has now become baseline digital literacy that most people under thirty take for granted. The term will probably age badly. But for now, it works.
